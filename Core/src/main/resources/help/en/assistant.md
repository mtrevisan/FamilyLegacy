# The Research Assistant

The Research Assistant reads the whole tree and points out what is wrong, doubtful or missing, and it offers helpers for the jobs that are hard to do by hand. It only suggests: **nothing changes unless you apply a suggestion**, and a draft is always yours to check.

## Opening it

- Press `Ctrl+Shift+A`, or choose **Tools > Research Assistant...**.
- A palette opens with a search field (*Search actions and diagnostics*). Type to filter, choose an entry, and press `Esc` to close. The search works as the one of *Find record*: it matches word by word (the words can be in the title, the category or the explanation, and an entry with more of them ranks higher), and the check boxes under the field narrow it to **Whole word** or widen it to typos (**Fuzzy**). With none of them chosen, the first letters of the words also match (`hda` finds *Historical Date Assistant*). The palette opens at once when the indicator has already computed the findings of the current data; it computes them only if the data changed.
- The **Assistant** indicator at the right end of the toolbar always shows the state of the tree: the number of errors, warnings and notes, or *no findings*. While the analysis runs it says *checking...*.

The palette has two sections:

- **Actions**: the helpers you can start.
- **Diagnostics**: the findings about your data, in three groups, **Errors**, **Warnings** and **Information**, each with its count; inside a group the findings of the same kind (e.g. *Possible duplicate places*) are listed together under their own heading, the more important ones first. The headings can be collapsed: double-click one, or choose it with the arrows and press `Enter`, to open or close it. The kinds start collapsed (the groups of errors, warnings and information start open), and while you search everything is open. Every finding belongs to a category: *Dates, Identity, Places, Sources, Relationships, Research, Conclusions, Context*. The whole list can be exported as a CSV file (separated by semicolons, as a spreadsheet in many countries expects) (`Ctrl+E`, *Export Findings...*): severity, category, kind, title, explanation, the identifiers of the records and whether the finding is ignored, to filter or compare it outside the application. The detail of a finding lists all its records, each with its type, what it is about and its identifier.

When you open the palette from the main window with an individual or a group selected, the findings **about that record come first**, and a check box under the search field (**Only about** *the record*) shows only them: it is the way to check the chronology, the relationships or the sources of one person. From an editor there is no such selection.

A finding can be opened (*Open the Records...*, to visit the records involved one after the other), its records can be followed to where they are used (`Ctrl+U`, *Where Used...*: for each record of the finding, the records that point to it and by which field, to see what depends on a duplicate or on a record with a problem; double-click one to open it) or **ignored** if you know it is not a problem. Ignored findings are remembered for the file, can be shown again, and can be restored.

## What it looks for

- **Chronology**: a death before the birth, an event before the birth or after the death, a child born before its parent or after the death of a parent, a parent too young or too old, children of the same mother born less than nine months apart (twins excepted), a marriage at too young an age, overlapping marriages, an implausible lifespan. The limits are conventions: a finding means "check this", not "this is wrong".
- **Conflicts**: an individual with two values for something that can have only one, several births, baptisms, deaths or burials with dates or places that cannot all be right (a town and its province agree), two biological mothers or fathers, someone who would be their own ancestor.
- **Identity**: two individuals that may be the same person, and two groups, places, sources, repositories, media, cultural norms or historic events that may be the same record, when no hypothesis or conclusion covers the pair yet. The comparison looks at names, dates, relatives, places, occupations and sources, by the records they share and, when they share none (a tree imported twice), by what they are called. A pair is a possibility, not a probability: nothing is merged. On a finding, **Enter** creates an identity hypothesis draft (with the sources both cite already in it), **Ctrl+Enter** compares the two records side by side, **Ctrl+Shift+Enter** records them as different (the editor of the hypothesis first, then, when you have saved it, a conclusion *disproven* whose narrative lists, feature by feature, why they looked alike and what tells them apart: the pair is not proposed again), and **Ctrl+Alt+Enter** opens the records. Each of these keys always does the same thing, in every finding: when a finding has no draft to propose, **Enter** simply does the same as **Ctrl+Enter** (the comparison). How many pairs are proposed and from which similarity is in **Settings > Identity Assistant...**, see [Preferences](preferences.md#identity-assistant).
- **Gaps in the research**: completed activities without a result, people in a census without a recorded residence, someone with no birth recorded, and similar holes. When the person is known at a place (an event other than the birth happened there) the finding says so (*Place known, birth record missing*) and the draft question suggests starting from the birth and baptism registers of that place and of the neighbouring ones.
- **Places**: places that may be the same place, a place that is part of nothing though its name says where it is, a parish with no diocese, a relationship that puts a larger place inside a smaller one, places that are part of each other in a circle, and a place where events happen that no source cites (*has no source*: the most used first; a place no event happens in is not reported). A relationship of places that ends before it begins is reported by **Validate File**, not here.
- **Context**: events that a historic event or a cultural norm of your model may have influenced, and clusters of deaths in a short time that nothing explains. They are possibilities, never statements: nothing is linked unless you create the impact.
- **Planning**: the next steps of an open research question (the records that would answer it and are not yet in the tree, not yet cited, and not yet covered by a task or an activity), and the *brick walls* worth working on: individuals whose parents are unknown, ranked by how much of the tree depends on them. They are ordered by an estimated usefulness, a rule of thumb and not a judgement of your research.
- **Consanguinity**: couples whose partners are related.

While the findings are being computed (it can take long on a large file) a bar at the bottom of the palette shows how far the analysis is, with the percentage in the status line; the commands are available at once and the findings appear when it is done.

For many findings the assistant offers an action such as **Create Conclusion Draft...**, a research question, a historic event, or a context impact. The draft lists the records involved and leaves the reasoning to you.

## The Historical Date Assistant

It opens from the date field of an editor, from the palette (**Actions > Dates**) and from **Tools > Historical Date Assistant...**. It interprets and converts, and **Apply to Date** puts the chosen suggestion in the date (you are asked before an existing value is replaced); opened from the Tools menu there is no date to apply to, so the button is not there and you can only read and copy the suggestions. Its tools:

- **Calendar Conversion**: converts a date between the Julian, Gregorian, Venetan, Florentine, Pisan and the other calendars. Under the fields a table writes the same day in the Gregorian, Julian, Hijrah (Islamic), Minguo, Thai Buddhist and Japanese calendars, with its Julian Day Number and the day of the week; the calendar you entered is marked with *, and **Copy All** puts the table in the clipboard. The table is filled only for a single day (not for a bare year). The Gregorian and Julian rows are the proleptic ones, without the reform of 1582; the Hijrah is the Umm al-Qura calendar (from 1882 to 2174); the Minguo years count from 1912 and the Japanese ones from 1873 (before that the months were lunisolar: use the tab with the Japanese calendar); the Thai Buddhist year began on 1 April until 1940. A calendar that cannot write the day says so in its row and does not hide the others. For a Julian day from 1 January to 24 March before the adoption of the Gregorian calendar in Great Britain (1752) it also gives the **dual date** (*12 February 1748/49*): where the year began on 25 March, as in England, Florence and Pisa, a source gives the previous year. It is informative only: the year start depends on the place, so it is not applied to the date. For the Gregorian reform you can choose Rome and the Catholic countries (1582), France (1582), Great Britain (1752), or the Julian calendar and the computus of the Orthodox churches.
- **Interval and Age**: the time between two dates, or two events of the tree, in years, months and days. The dates can be in any calendar and the second one starts as today, so with a birth as the first date the result is the age today. The order does not matter: if the second date is before the first one the interval is counted backwards. If a date is not a single day (a bare year, an approximate date) the result is the closest and the farthest possible interval.
- **Age Estimator**: estimates the date of birth from the age at death, marriage or another event. The result is a range, not a false precise date: *died on 3 May 1900 aged 72* means born between 4 May 1827 and 3 May 1828. The age can be given in completed years or as the ordinal of the year of life (*in his 72nd year*). A date written as a bare number is the civil year only for the Julian and Gregorian calendars; for any other calendar it is the year in the calendar you chose (`5786` in the Hebrew calendar is that whole Hebrew year). An empty date is rejected.
- **Several Ages**: narrows the birth by intersecting the ages stated in several records (death, marriage, census...). Add each statement with **Add Clue** and press **Compute**: the result is the range of days allowed by all of them, or, if they contradict one another, the clues that are incompatible and by how many days. All the clues are assumed to refer to the same person.
- **Verify a Date**: checks the **weekday** and the **indiction** that a source gives for a date. A wrong weekday is a transcription clue: the tool gives the nearest day with that weekday and says if the other civil calendar (Julian instead of Gregorian, or the other way round) would have matched. The indiction is checked against the date in every style (the Greek style changes on 1 September, the Bedan one on 24 September, the Roman ones on 25 December or 1 January), because the style is not written in the document; a year alone is checked over the whole year. If it does not match, the tool tells which indiction the date has and the years around it that have the stated one. In the lower part, **Years of an indiction** gives, for an indiction and an interval of years, the years that have it (one in fifteen).
- **Relative Date Calculator**: a date a number of days, weeks, months or years before or after another one (*nine months before the baptism*). Months and years are counted in the calendar keeping the day of the month; a day that does not exist (31 February) becomes the last day of the month.
- **Date Relative to an Event**: the same, measured from an event of the tree.
- **Liturgical Date Calculator**: Easter, Pentecost, Advent and the other movable feasts, feast-day references such as *Michaelmas 1498* or *3 days after Easter 1400*, and the distance from Easter. The reform you choose is honoured also when you type a feast reference (Rome, France, Great Britain, or the Julian calendar and the Orthodox computus), or one of the historical jurisdictions listed after them (the Austrian lands, the Dutch provinces, the Swiss cantons, Bohemia, Hungary...): several countries did not switch on a single day, so there is no "Austria" or "Switzerland" entry, only the lands that really changed on their own dates. If your file has places, the **Place** list picks the reform for you: a place is matched through its own name and the names (also the alternative ones) of the places that contain it, nearest first, so a town contained in *Tyrol*, contained in *Austria*, gets the reform of Tyrol. Only places whose container chain names a known jurisdiction are listed; for the others, choose the reform by hand. When you open the assistant from the date of an event that has a place, the reform of that place is already selected; it is only a starting point, and you can choose any other reform (or another place) at any time. *Holy Thursday* is Maundy Thursday, the Thursday before Easter (the Ascension is *Ascension Day* or *Ascension Thursday*).
- **Roman Date (Kalends, Nones, Ides)**: a day written the Roman way (*ante diem III Nonas Martias*, *pridie Kalendas Ianuarias*, *Idibus Octobribus*, *the Ides of March*) in the year you give. The words are understood in Latin, with the usual abbreviations (*a.d. XVI Kal. Mart.*), and the Kalends, Nones and Ides also in English; the old names of July and August, *Quintilis* and *Sextilis*, are recognised too. The year is the one of the day itself, so *pridie Kalendas Ianuarias* of 1501 is 31 December 1501. In a leap year the sixth day before the Kalends of March was doubled in classical practice: for the days of February from the 24th backwards the assistant gives both readings (the leap day added at the end, and the doubled sixth day), and *ante diem bis sextum* is read as the 24th, the first of the two sixth days, while the plain sixth day (*a.d. VI Kal. Mart.*, the feast of Saint Matthias) is the 25th. A day that does not exist (for example *ante diem VII Nonas Ianuarias*) is rejected.
- **Sunrise and Sunset**: the sunrise and sunset at a place, and what they mean for an event given in hours from sunset. A date in the Julian or Gregorian calendar keeps its calendar; a date of any other calendar is read in the calendar in force at that day. A calendar the tool does not know is an error, not silently read as Gregorian. The place is given as latitude and longitude, or chosen with the **Map...** button: it opens the same map as the chronomap, centered on the coordinates already entered, with the chronomap timeline at the bottom (it starts on the date entered: move it to choose the year) and the historical maps that depict that year (chosen automatically, or by hand from the **Historical Map** button); click on the place and confirm, and its coordinates fill the fields. This one needs the **Ephemeris plugin**; without it the assistant says so.

Every tool has its own **Compute** button (at the right of its fields). The suggestions appear in the list under the tabs; the area below explains the selected one in four parts: how the input was *interpreted*, the *assumptions* it relies on, the *calculation* step by step and the *result*, with the calendar it is written in. **Copy Explanation** puts this text in the clipboard, to paste it in a note. Errors are shown in red under the explanation.

The results are suggestions to support your research, not an authority: check the assumptions before applying one.

## The Source Citation Assistant

It is offered only while you are inside a citation editor, and it is described in full, with the words it understands and examples, in [The Source Citation Assistant](citation-assistant.md). In short: it turns an archival reference such as *vol. 3, p. 45, n. 12* into the **locator** of a citation. It also shows clues taken from the media of the source and the repositories named in the reference. **Apply to Citation** writes the locator (after asking, if there is one already), and **Copy Locator** puts it on the clipboard. What it did not understand is listed, so nothing is lost silently. The locator is a draft built from your text: check it before applying it.
